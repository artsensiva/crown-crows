# Crow calls: should the box play "crow phrases"? (research, 2026-11-21)

Question: is there research on what crows "say", are there ready phrases in crow language that would help to train them, and should the box get a speaker?

**Short answer: no ready phrases exist, and a speaker with recorded crow calls is not recommended now.** If a sound is wanted at all, the evidence-based use is a plain, neutral sound at the moment of the reward (like a clicker), not crow speech. Method: a web search of papers, preprints and news (not full-text reading of every paper; where only a summary was seen, it is said so).

## What is known about crow calls

- **Calls are being mapped, not translated.** A 2025 field study put 52 lightweight tags (MiniDTAG) on wild, cooperatively breeding carrion crows in northern Spain and collected over 127,000 vocalisations, with a machine-learning classifier sorting them from long-distance calls to quiet murmurs. Many calls are quiet and at close range inside the family; the project states that decoding *meaning* is the goal and still at an early stage ([Earth Species Project, Dec 2025](https://earthspecies.org/2025/12/15/unlocking-avian-secrets-how-tiny-biologgers-are-revealing-the-hidden-communication-of-carrion-crows/); [Animal Cognition](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10071-025-02018-0); [Mongabay, May 2026](https://news.mongabay.com/2026/05/from-caws-to-code-ai-helps-decrypt-animal-communication/)).
- **Function by context** (2026 preprint, 24 groups): quiet close-range calls seem to coordinate chick care by announcing nest visits ([bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.04.02.715916v1.full)). A systematic review of 130 corvid studies finds that calls carry information about predators and food and serve group cohesion ([Animal Behaviour review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003347224003841)). An older thesis found most carrion crow calls on a continuum of loud calls rather than clearly separate "words" ([University of Leicester](https://figshare.le.ac.uk/articles/thesis/Aspects_of_vocal_communication_in_the_carrion_crow_Corvus_corone_corone_/10162910)).
- **Crows control their voice.** Three carrion crows were trained to produce one to four calls on cue, planning the sequence before calling ([Liao, Brecht, Veit, Nieder, Science 2024](https://uni-tuebingen.de/en/university/news-and-publications/press-release/crows-can-deliberately-plan-how-many-calls-to-make/)). That says something about their minds, not about a vocabulary we could use.
- **Call structure follows regularities** (longer sequences contain shorter calls, Menzerath's law; [Vocal efficiency in crows, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40742461/)), and the scolding response to a dangerous person or a dead crow is *learned* and spreads between crows (work of the Marzluff lab, American crows: [one](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3234554), [two](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4417708)); one site saw it spread over at least 1.2 km in five years.

## Does playing crow calls attract or train wild crows? Mixed evidence

| Call played | Result (as far as the sources show) |
|---|---|
| American crow food-area calls (short calls near a big food source) | Playback did not make crows find food more often; a mildly aggressive response ([Animal Behaviour 2019, abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0003347219300363)) |
| Crow assembly calls | Wild crows gathered at the speaker in about a quarter of trials (figure quoted in a secondary source, [Corvid Research blog](https://corvidresearch.blog/2019/03/14/crow-vocalizations-part-i-new-science/); original not checked) |
| Raven "haa" food calls | Ravens are drawn strongly, and the response depends on who the caller is ([raven study, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4289921/)) |
| Alarm or dead-crow related calls | Crows respond with mobbing, avoid the place and learn from one exposure ([Swift and Marzluff](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4417708)) |

Nothing found shows that a recorded "phrase" makes crows learn *this box*. A search for other devices found one precedent: the 2008 crow vending machine, whose claims corvid researchers disputed ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2008/03/04/87878028/inventor-trains-crows-to-find-money); [Kottke](https://kottke.org/09/04/no-vending-machine-for-crows)), so it is no evidence either way.

## What crows do learn quickly: arbitrary sounds tied to rewards

Captive crows learn sound-to-reward and sound-to-picture associations quickly, including a short feedback sound followed by a feeder (Nieder lab studies, for example [Moll and Nieder 2015](https://homepage.uni-tuebingen.de/andreas.nieder/Moll,%20Nieder%20%282015%29%20CurrBiol.pdf), a short sound marking correct trials with a feeder reward in the search summaries; full texts not read). Those were laboratory birds; a neutral chime played when the reward drops is an *inference* for wild crows, not a tested result. It needs no crow recording and cannot be mistaken for an alarm.

## Risks of playing recorded wild-bird calls

- **Alarm, scolding or dead-crow calls could teach crows to avoid the box** (the learned scolding above spreads through a population). Never play them.
- Ethics guidance discourages playback near breeding birds: Audubon and the American Birding Association advise limiting it, BirdLife Australia suggests no more than three 30 s bouts in 5 minutes at one site ([Audubon](https://www.audubon.org/magazine/why-photographers-should-reconsider-using-playback-field); [BirdLife Australia guidelines](https://direct.birdlife.org.au/documents/POL-Ethical-Birding-Guidelines.pdf)). A food box is not a territorial lure, but a call played all day near nests is the situation those guidelines warn about. Neighbours and local rules about noise also apply.
- A speaker is more code, more parts and another current peak on a supply that already shows a dip at every servo stroke. Audio was switched off on the Pi on 21 Nov (`dtparam=audio=off`, [HISTORY](HISTORY.md)); a speaker would need it back on or a small I2S amplifier.

## Recommendation

1. **Do not install a speaker with crow phrases now.** There is no validated vocabulary, the evidence for attraction is mixed, and the risks (alarm calls, noise, stress to nesting birds) and the cost in code and energy are real.
2. **If the owner wants to try a sound, run a small controlled test:** a short neutral chime (not a crow sound) at the moment of the reward, low volume, a very small amount of code, on alternate weeks (sound on / off), and compare the number of visits and returning crows per day from the visit log. Keep it only if visits rise.
3. **Only after that, and only if wanted,** try one recorded food-associated or contact call from the same species, in the same on/off design, never alarm or mobbing calls.
4. Better returns for the training are probably elsewhere: food placement, the 25 mm intake height, and time (the 2008 precedent and the learning literature agree that patient shaping with food matters more than sound).

## If a speaker is ever added: the cheapest efficient option (owner's decision 2026-11-21: no sound test now; kept as a future technology)

|---|---|---|---|
| **Recommended: I2S class-D amplifier MAX98357A + a small 4 Ω speaker** | module about 5-6 € or US$ ([Adafruit 3006, US$5.95](https://www.adafruit.com/products/3006); [DFRobot DFR0954, about £5](https://thepihut.com/collections/dfrobot/products/max98357-i2s-amplifier-module)) plus a 4 Ω 40 mm speaker of a few euros (price not checked) | 2.4 mA quiescent, about 12 mW at 5 V ([Analog Devices](https://www.analog.com/en/products/MAX98357A.html)); the shutdown pin (`SD`) can be driven by a GPIO to bring it near zero (the shutdown current of this exact part was not confirmed in the sources); output 3.2 W into 4 Ω at 5 V, a short chime uses a fraction of that | Plays any recorded sound, no audio jack needed. **Conflict:** it uses GPIO 18, 19 and 21, and the servo's hardware PWM is on GPIO 18 ([I2S pins and the PWM conflict](https://learn.adafruit.com/adafruit-max98357-i2s-class-d-mono-amp/pinouts?view=all)). The servo would move to GPIO 12 (`dtoverlay=pwm,pin=12,func=4`, a wire to move and to check on the bench), the amplifier uses `dtoverlay=max98357a` |
| **Cheapest, tones only: passive piezo buzzer or small speaker on a free GPIO through a transistor** | below 1 € (my own engineering suggestion, not from a source) | zero when silent; a short beep draws a few tens of mA | No recorded sounds, only beeps made by the program; no I2S, no pin conflict, about 10 lines of code |
| Not recommended: USB speaker or the 3.5 mm jack | a USB sound card with a powered speaker | the USB device draws power all the time (hundreds of mW) | The jack's PWM audio may clash with the servo's PWM, and audio was switched off on 21 Nov to save energy |

**Advice:** for a plain chime at the reward use the piezo (cheapest, nothing to conflict). For recorded sounds of any kind use the MAX98357A with the `SD` pin on a GPIO and the servo moved to GPIO 12. Whatever the choice: low volume, played only at the reward, never alarm or mobbing sounds (see above). Before buying, check the pin plan on the bench box ([HARDWARE](HARDWARE.md)); the part is also marked by its maker as superseded for new designs by the MAX98365.

### Options seen in a shop listing (Funduino via Kaufland, 2026-11-21, prices with VAT, shipping 4.90 € extra)

- **Miniature speaker 3 W, 4 Ω, 31 × 28 mm, JST PH 1.25 cable, 2.94 €** ([listing](https://www.kaufland.de/product/540123492/)): a suitable speaker for the MAX98357A option; it is only a speaker (no amplifier), the tiny connector has to be cut off to reach the amplifier terminals, 3 W is the limit of the amplifier so the volume must be limited in software, and a 31 mm speaker is thin in the low notes (fine for a chime). Not weatherproof: inside the box.
- **Simplest, no extra power: an active buzzer module with a 3-pin XH2.54 socket** (5 V, digital signal, 34 × 22 × 12 mm; about 3.4-3.7 € in the same shop; [shop page](https://funduinoshop.com/en/components/active-components/buzzer-and-loudspeaker/active-loudspeaker-with-jst-xh2.54-3p-socket)). It makes its own beep as long as the signal is high, so the program needs about 5 lines: `LED(24).on()`, wait 0.3 s, `off()`. Wiring: `+` to a 5 V pin of the Pi, `−` to a ground pin, signal to a free GPIO (GPIO24, physical pin 18; GPIO17, 18, 22, 23, 27 and 19 are taken, see [HARDWARE](HARDWARE.md)). No extra supply and no amplifier. To check when it arrives: whether the module has its own driver transistor; if not, one BC337 transistor (about 0.30 €) between the GPIO and the module protects the pin (a buzzer draws more than a GPIO may give). Energy: zero when silent, about 0.15 W for 0.3 s when it beeps. Only a beep, no recorded sounds; the same shop's "passive speaker" module (1.5-15 V, tones of about 1.5-2.5 kHz) allows different tones but needs a PWM tone from the program.

### The buzzer module and the crows' hearing (owner's question, 2026-11-21)

- The listing's title says "Active speaker - with amplifier", but its technical data table says **"Digitally controlled active piezo buzzer", 3.5-5.5 V, rated current 0.03 A** (the long description even speaks of an analog audio signal). The table fits a buzzer that beeps at a fixed pitch while its signal is high, so that is what to expect; ask the seller or try it on arrival before wiring it for anything else ([product page](https://funduino.com/en/products/active-speaker-with-jst-xh2-54-3p-socket)).
- Crow hearing: a historical audiogram (Trainer 1946, the only crow-specific figures found) puts the American crow's range at about 0.3-8 kHz with the best sensitivity around 2-3 kHz ([Auk 1978, plotting that audiogram](https://sora.unm.edu/sites/default/files/journals/auk/v095n03/p0563-p0568.pdf); the values were read from a garbled table). Birds in general hear best between about 1 and 5 kHz. Small piezo buzzers usually sound at 2-4 kHz, so the pitch is already in the crows' best range; a special construction is not needed for audibility.
- What helps without building anything: point the buzzer's sound opening at the landing place (high tones are directional), do not bury it in a closed housing (a hole of a few millimetres in front of the opening), mount it firmly so the housing does not rattle, and check the loudness at the crow's position with a phone sound-level app (loud enough to be heard clearly over wind and traffic, not startling). Weather: keep it dry inside the box.

### "Crows talk at low frequencies": what that means for the speaker (2026-11-21)

- **What was found.** The carrion crow's basic caw has a fundamental near 1.5 kHz falling to about 1.3 kHz, with harmonics above and below (one hobbyist source with sonograms, [Wild Echoes](https://www.wildechoes.org/species/crows-magpies-jays/carrion-crow); no peer-reviewed carrion crow spectrum was found); American crow caws were measured in several contexts ([PMC4237024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4237024)). That is low *for a bird* (songbirds sing at 4-8 kHz) but it is the middle of the audio range, 0.5-4 kHz, not bass. No study shows corvids hearing or communicating in infrasound (below 20 Hz); infrasound hearing is documented for pigeons and a few other species ([review of avian infrasound hearing](https://www.knmi.nl/research/publications/infrasonic-hearing-in-birds-a-review-of-audiometry-and-hypothesized-structure-function-relationships)). The only crow audiogram found (1946) has the best sensitivity at 2-3 kHz.
- **Conclusions.** (1) For a *training signal* (a sound that only means "reward now") the pitch is not critical: it must be clearly audible, always the same and clearly different from the noises of the place. A 2-4 kHz buzzer is audible to a crow and the cheapest and most efficient (high loudness per watt, because a piezo buzzer works at its resonance). Low notes would need a bigger speaker, more power and more money, with no evidence that they train better. (2) A rhythm that does not occur in nature, for example two short beeps, makes the sound more distinctive at no cost (an inference, not a tested result). (3) Only if the box is ever to play crow-like calls does the speaker have to reach down to about 400-500 Hz: a 31 mm miniature speaker is weak below roughly 600-800 Hz (general acoustics, not measured); a 40-57 mm speaker in a small closed housing with the MAX98357A would be needed (the shop listing has 8 Ω 0.5 W for 1.30 € and 5 W 30 × 70 mm for 1.84 €; sound quality not checked).
- **Cheap plan.** Stage 1: the buzzer module (about 3.4 €) with a distinctive two-beep pattern at the exact moment of the reward, the same way every time and only for rewards (a delay of more than about a second weakens the link). Stage 2, only if the owner wants crow-like sounds: MAX98357A (about 5 €) and a 40-57 mm speaker (about 2 €), total about 8 €, and only after the stage-1 results.
